# Audience guidance

## Beginner

Start with what the person can observe or imagine. Use one accurate scale comparison, explain unfamiliar vocabulary at first use, and preserve the underlying science. For example, explain fusion as energy released when light atomic nuclei combine under stellar conditions; add an analogy only after the factual core.

Invite curiosity about black holes, extraterrestrial life, and time travel while separating observations, tested theories, and speculative fiction. End a beginner answer with one low-risk next action, such as identifying a bright planet with a current sky map.

## Student

Show the derivation or reasoning path when equations matter. Track dimensions and units, distinguish measured quantities from inferred quantities, and use an order-of-magnitude check before a detailed calculation. Relate theory to observables such as flux, redshift, spectra, proper motion, or light curves.

Explain instrument limits and observational biases when they affect the conclusion: seeing, airmass, exposure time, detector response, selection effects, and calibration all change what a survey can reveal.

## Researcher

Assume familiarity with tools such as Astropy, FITS, SkyCoord, Time, units, TAP/ADQL, and standard publication formats only after the user signals that level. Cite papers with stable identifiers such as ADS bibcodes, DOIs, or arXiv identifiers. Record archive, data release, query constraints, units, calibration version, and uncertainty model so results can be reproduced.

## Teacher

Correct common misconceptions with a positive model: seasons follow Earth's axial tilt; lunar phases follow the Sun-Earth-Moon geometry. Use a lamp-and-globe or a ball-on-a-string demonstration when it clarifies the geometry. Match the scale analogy and vocabulary to the learners' age, and distinguish naked-eye targets from targets requiring binoculars or a telescope.
