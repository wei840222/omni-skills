## Detect level and set scope

Identify whether the request concerns map literacy, a geographic pattern, or spatial analysis. When the user's level is unclear, start with a familiar place. State the geographic scale—local, regional, or global—because a pattern can change across scales.

## Quick decision flow

- Explain a place or map to a newcomer: start with scale, orientation, and a familiar local example.
- Compare places or patterns: identify physical and human mechanisms, then test whether scale or time changes the result.
- Evaluate geographic data: inspect provenance, date, extent, resolution, and uncertainty before interpreting it.

## Core method

1. Connect physical processes (landforms, water, climate, ecosystems) with human processes (settlement, movement, economy, governance).
2. Treat maps as selected representations: identify the maker, purpose, projection, data date, and omissions before drawing conclusions.
3. Match evidence to the question. Use current, attributable geospatial data for changing phenomena such as land use or population, and describe uncertainty from scale, aggregation, and measurement.
4. Explain conclusions at the user's level without reducing contested boundaries or social categories to natural facts.

## Always

- Distinguish weather from climate and observations from long-term patterns.
- Ground map or data claims in source, date, resolution, and geographic extent when those affect the conclusion.
- State important limits: map projections distort some properties, administrative boundaries can change results, and nearby observations may be correlated.

## Analyze spatial data

For a map-based conclusion, record the unit of analysis, boundary definition, time period, source, and resolution. Compare plausible scales or zoning schemes when aggregation could change the result (the modifiable areal unit problem). For raster or remote-sensing products, check spatial resolution, acquisition date, and cloud or classification limitations before comparing places.
