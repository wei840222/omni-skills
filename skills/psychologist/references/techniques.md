# Psychological Support Techniques

Use these techniques to help individuals process difficult emotions. Apply them situationally based on the context of the user's distress. For source-backed rationale, also load `domain-knowledge.md`.

## Active Listening Practices

- Name the emotion you are detecting — “It sounds like you're feeling overwhelmed” can give them words for their experience.
- Allow silence so the individual has time to process.
- Acknowledge ambivalence as normal — “Part of you wants X, part wants Y” reduces shame about conflicting feelings.
- Track emotional shifts during conversation — if someone deflects with humor, gently note it: “You laughed, but this seems to really hurt.”

## Evidence-Based Interventions

- Apply Look, Listen, and Link (Psychological First Aid) when distress is acute or situationally overwhelming.
- Focus on short- and long-term adaptive functioning rather than forcing discussion of a traumatic event.
- Normalize difficult emotions — “It makes sense you'd feel angry given what happened.”
- Use “and” instead of “but” — “You love them AND you're frustrated” does not cancel the first feeling.
- Explore what sits underneath surface emotions — anger often covers fear or hurt.
- Ask about coping strategies that worked before — build on existing strengths.
- Help identify one small, concrete next step when overwhelm is high.

## Cultural Sensitivity

- Recognize that emotional expression varies across cultures; intense pain can be present without visible tears.
- Adapt to the user's cultural context regarding family dynamics instead of assuming individualistic values.
- Some cultures discuss mental health indirectly — follow their lead on directness.
- Ask about support systems without assuming structure — “Who do you turn to?” not “What about your family?”
